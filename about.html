✦ Story

How it started

I never started with a course or a plan. I started with a problem I wanted solved and a browser tab full of tutorials. Each small win, a button that finally worked or a bot that finally replied, made the next problem feel possible.

Learning alone taught me what no syllabus can: how to get unstuck.

Reading docs, breaking things, fixing them and writing down what worked became a habit that still runs everything I make. Slowly the experiments grew into real products people could open and use: web tools, Telegram bots, Android apps and the visual design that ties them together.

Today I split my time between building new tools, polishing the old ones, and sharing what I learn so others can skip the mistakes I made.

✦ Process

How I build

  1. 1
    Listen first

    Before any code, I want to know who this is for and what they are trying to get done. The best technology choice usually becomes obvious after that.

  2. 2
    Build the smallest real version

    A rough but working prototype teaches more in a day than a perfect plan does in a week. It shows what is missing while it is still cheap to change.

  3. 3
    Polish what people touch

    Speed, spacing, wording and error messages get my full attention, because that is the part users actually feel.

  4. 4
    Ship, then listen again

    I release early, watch how people really use it, and improve from behaviour instead of guesses.

✦ Mindset

Questions I ask first

Who is this really for?

Every good tool has one clear person in mind. If I can’t describe them in a single sentence, I’m not ready to build yet.

What is the one job it must do well?

One thing done perfectly beats ten things done halfway. Everything else can wait for version two.

Will it feel smooth on a slow phone?

Many people use my work on mobile, often on weak connections. If it is smooth there, it is smooth everywhere.

What happens when something goes wrong?

Errors and empty states are part of the design. A calm, helpful message earns more trust than a flawless happy path.

Could someone else maintain this?

I write for the person who opens the code next, and that person is very often future me.
